Welcome to Coffee Break on the Field. Let's start with last year.

Rewind to the trade deadline twelve months ago. The Kansas City Chiefs wanted Breece Hall, the New York Jets running back.

Connor Hughes of SNY reported it on deadline day, and here is exactly what he said.

Quote. "There was an offer on the table for a fourth-round pick for Breece Hall."

And then, quote. "The Jets were steadfast in wanting a third."

And then the ending, quote. "They were not able to get one. Only had a fourth-round offer."

One round. That is what killed it. Kansas City said fourth, New York said third, and nobody blinked, and Breece Hall stayed in New York.

Now look at where we are this week.

Adam Schefter reported that the Indianapolis Colts have told teams they are looking for third round value for Josh Downs. And then Schefter added, quote, "Nobody's willing to do that right now."

Same standoff. Same round. Same general manager.

So when I told you on Tuesday that my honest prediction was a modest move rather than a star, this is the evidence behind it. That was not pessimism about Brett Veach. It is a pattern with a receipt attached, from a real reporter, on a real deadline day, about a real player this team actually wanted.

But I promised you honest, so let me give you the counter-argument, because there is a strong one and I think it matters.

Last year, Breece Hall was a luxury. Kansas City had a functioning running game and was trying to upgrade it. This year is completely different. Tyquan Thornton is gone for the season, Rashee Rice has a soft tissue history, and the wide receivers rank thirty-first out of thirty-two in share of the team's receiving yards. That is not a want. That is a hole.

A general manager who will not pay a third-round pick for an upgrade will sometimes pay one for a hole. And remember what Andy Reid said on Wednesday, in his own words, when asked whether Veach had started bringing him names. Quote, "Yeah, about a month ago."

A month of work. That is a front office that already knows every price in the league. The question is only whether it is willing to meet one.

And there is one more wrinkle in that Breece Hall story that I think is the most interesting part, and almost nobody mentions it.

Breece Hall was never traded. The Jets kept him, and the reporter who broke the story said at the time that not trading Hall meant the Jets then needed to extend him. So a year later, Kansas City does not have Breece Hall, the Jets still do, and the running back Kansas City eventually got instead was Kenneth Walker the third, who came in free agency rather than in a trade and who is still, right now, one of the more productive backs in football.

Which is a reminder that a deal falling apart is not automatically a failure. Sometimes the discipline that looks like timidity in November looks like patience in March. I am not saying that is what happened. I am saying it is a possible reading and I would be a coward not to mention it while I am using the same story to predict caution.

Now, this morning's ESPN column from Dan Graziano and Jeremy Fowler, because there is a lot in it.

Start with the Colts, who are running a genuine fire sale. Graziano lists the players likely to move, and the list is longer than you think.

Josh Downs, wide receiver. Likely to move. And Graziano added the detail that actually governs his price. He is twenty-five years old and eligible for free agency at the end of this season.

Let me explain why that single fact is the whole argument against paying a third-round pick for him.

When you trade for a player on an expiring contract, you are not buying a player. You are buying nine regular season games, a playoff run if you get one, and then an extension negotiation in which you have no leverage, because he can simply leave. You have rented him. And a rental at receiver, when the deadline is this week, is nine Sundays and a conversation in March.

That is why a third-round pick is too much, and it is why the entire league has said no, and it is why Indianapolis will almost certainly have to come down.

Second Colts name, and this one is new. Keenan Allen.

Graziano lists Keenan Allen among the Colts likely to move. Indianapolis signed him in late August, and he is one of the most accomplished route runners of the last decade. No destination and no price have been reported, so I am telling you the name and nothing more. But if you are looking for a cheap, savvy, short-area veteran who has caught a thousand passes against every coverage in football, he is now technically on the market.

Third name. Mo Alie-Cox, tight end, also drawing interest according to that column. I mention him for completeness rather than because I expect anything.

And now the fourth name, which is the one I think Kansas City fans should actually pay attention to, and almost nobody is talking about it.

Grover Stewart. Defensive tackle.

Graziano says Stewart is likely to move and expects him to land with a new team within about a week, because several teams are looking for interior defensive linemen.

Now connect that to this football team. Kansas City has nine sacks in seven games. Peter Woods, the first-round rookie defensive tackle, has a neck stinger and did not practice Wednesday or Thursday. Omarr Norman-Lott is still limited and the team is taking the full twenty-one day window before deciding on him. And Chris Jones, who carries the largest cap charge on the roster, finished the Denver game with no tackles and no sacks.

Everybody in Kansas City is talking about receivers. The interior of that defensive line is quietly just as thin.

And here is the catch, and Graziano reported the number, which is exactly the kind of detail that makes a column worth reading. Grover Stewart's base salary this season is eleven point seven four million dollars, and an acquiring team would need to fit roughly half of that under its salary cap.

Half of eleven point seven four is about five point nine million. Kansas City has roughly one point two five million in space.

So that is the proration I explained to you earlier this week. You do not take on the whole annual salary in a mid-season trade, you take on the part that has not been paid yet, which by week nine is roughly half. And half of Grover Stewart is still almost five times what Kansas City currently has available.

Which means a Stewart trade requires one of two things. Either Indianapolis converts most of that salary into a signing bonus and eats the cap charge itself, which sellers do when they want picks badly enough, or Kansas City restructures contracts to create room. Both are possible. Neither is free.

And the last Colts name is the biggest. Jonathan Taylor.

Indianapolis is open to dealing him and is asking for a lot. Fowler's sources value Taylor at a second-round pick plus an additional pick, while Indianapolis may want something closer to the equivalent of a first-rounder. An AFC executive put it beautifully, in four words, quote, "They want a bunch."

Graziano expects Taylor to be traded, and he floats the New York Giants as a possible destination.

Now the receiver market beyond the Colts, with every price Graziano and Fowler actually reported.

Brian Thomas Junior. The Chiefs and the Steelers are both interested. And here is the new and important nuance. Several teams around the league are confident that Jacksonville will eventually move him. But the Jaguars have not yet shown any willingness to do it.

And the column says the situation may clarify after Jacksonville plays Baltimore on Thursday. Which is a very specific thing to say, and what it means is that one more bad offensive afternoon might change a front office's mind.

Calvin Ridley. He is viewed around the league as the backup option for teams that miss out on the higher-priced targets. And now we have an actual price. Tennessee would reportedly need something like a fifth-round pick.

A fifth. That is the cheapest real name on the board, and it is worth remembering when people tell you Kansas City will do nothing. A fifth-round pick is not a decision that requires courage.

And two names I can now take off the board for you.

George Pickens. Teams have asked the Cowboys about him. But Fowler's sources say Pickens wants to stay in Dallas and the Cowboys do not plan to trade him. So if you see a Pickens to Kansas City thumbnail this weekend, you now know more than the person who made it.

Hollywood Brown. Graziano sees him as a possible piece if Philadelphia decides to sell, but he does not expect a major sell-off from the Eagles. Which, by the way, also quietly lowers the odds on the Jordan Mailata story we covered.

And one more piece of context that raises the stakes on everything. The Pittsburgh Steelers, who are bidding against Kansas City on both Downs and Thomas, have Michael Pittman Junior and Roman Wilson on injured reserve, and DK Metcalf has been struggling.

That is a desperate team. And desperate teams raise prices.

Now, the actual football debate, and this is where the version of this story I was sent did its best work, so I want to give it full credit and then tell you where I land.

The argument was this. Josh Downs is a short-area slot operator. In many ways, he is what Kansas City already has. The phrase used was, oh no, another Xavier Worthy. And the person making the argument did not dodge it. He said yes, that is what Downs is.

But then he said the thing that I think is exactly right, and I am going to quote the shape of it because it is a genuinely good sentence. He said Downs could raise the floor of the offense even if he does not change its ceiling.

That is the whole case for Josh Downs in eleven words. He is better at the short-area job than the men currently doing it. He wins quickly off the line, he finds the soft spots against zone, and he turns short catches into longer gains, which are precisely the skills that decide third downs. And third down is where drives live and die.

And against that, the argument for Brian Thomas Junior. The trade market has very few genuine deep threats. Thomas has not been playing well lately, but at his best he is an elite vertical weapon, and that is the specific thing this offense has not had since Tyquan Thornton went down.

Now let me make the deep threat argument properly, because the ripple effect is bigger than most people realize and it is worth understanding.

A receiver who can genuinely run past a defense forces the safeties to stay deep. And once your safeties are deep, four separate things happen to you, all at once.

One. The intermediate middle of the field opens up, which is where Travis Kelce lives.

Two. The underneath routes open up, which is where the running backs catch the ball, and we have just watched Emmett Johnson turn a check-down into seventy-three yards.

Three. The box gets lighter. A defense that has to keep two safeties back cannot put as many bodies on the line of scrimmage, which means the run game faces fewer defenders, which is the exact opposite of what Seattle and Denver did to Kenneth Walker.

And four. Patrick Mahomes becomes more dangerous when he leaves the pocket, because defenders now have to honor deep and short at the same time while a man is improvising.

That is why a vertical threat is worth more than his own statistics suggest. He does not just produce. He changes the geometry for everybody else.

And here is the asymmetry that I think should settle the price question, and the story I was sent raised it and I want to make it sharper.

Brian Thomas Junior is in the third year of a four-year rookie contract with a fifth-year team option. Josh Downs is a free agent in two months.

So one of them is an asset you control into twenty twenty-eight, and the other is a rental for nine games.

Which means the entire debate about whether Kansas City should be willing to pay one more round of draft capital is slightly the wrong question. The right question is whether it should pay more for the player it keeps, and the answer to that is obviously yes.

But, and this is where I have to be honest rather than clever, Jacksonville reportedly does not want to trade him to an AFC contender. Kansas City is an AFC contender. So the better player may be unavailable at any price, and the worse fit may be the only one actually purchasable.

So here is where I land. On football, I want Brian Thomas Junior. On probability, I expect Josh Downs or nothing. And the right process is the one that story described perfectly: define the need, set a price ceiling, and be willing to hit that ceiling if the market comes to it. Teams that overpay for rentals regret it. Teams that hesitate miss the fix that costs them in January. Discipline is not the same thing as timidity, and the difference between the two is whether you decided your number in advance.

Now, two other things from that press conference, and I want to address them fairly because they are about judgment rather than facts.

First, the kicker. Harrison Butker stays. Andy Reid said, quote, "Harrison will be up, yeah." Justin Tucker remains on the practice squad and will not take over.

The criticism in the story I was sent was that Reid continues to make excuses for Butker despite poor performance, and that this is the Andy Reid's boys phenomenon, where if you are one of his guys the rope never runs out.

Let me give you both sides.

The number is bad. Harrison Butker has missed sixteen kicks since the start of last season, which is the most of any kicker in the National Football League over that span. That is not a slump, that is a trend with a sample size.

But the breakdown matters and I keep coming back to it. He is nine of ten from inside fifty yards this season. Ninety percent. What he has lost is the long one, where he is two of six. So the honest version is that Kansas City has a kicker who is reliable at normal distances and a coin flip from fifty-plus, which argues less for replacing him and more for not asking him to try fifty-four yarders in the rain.

And the story made a point about the position itself that I thought was genuinely wise. Kicking is unusual because confidence is part of the mechanism. A struggling kicker can be repaired with technique, routine and time, or he can spiral. Keeping faith is a real strategy, not just sentiment.

But the other half of that is also true, and the story said it well. When a team has a viable alternative sitting on its own roster and chooses not to use him, it is making a statement about trust. That statement may be correct. It also means the margin for error is now very thin, and if the misses continue, that decision gets judged in a close game.

Second, Kingsley Suamataia, and the story made a distinction I thought was sharp.

Reid refuses to call the benching a punishment. And the suggestion was that in Reid's mind it may be discipline, which is a different word with a different meaning. Punishment is retribution for something that happened. Discipline is a standard being enforced.

That is a careful and fair reading. I will add one thing to it, though, which cuts against even that. Reid did not only decline to call it punishment. He specifically said it was, quote, "not a disciplinarian issue."

So he has now ruled out both words. Which means the honest answer remains the one I gave you earlier in the week. Nobody outside that building knows, the head coach has said it is between him and the player, and the man is likely starting on Sunday.

And now the thing from that story that I want to endorse completely, because it is about how to do this job and I think the person who said it deserves to hear it back.

He said, and I am paraphrasing closely, that respect and criticism are not opposites. That a coach with a long record of success deserves credit for it and also deserves honest evaluation when decisions look questionable. And then he said this, which is the part I want to underline. He said he thinks Reid can be criticized without anyone calling for his head. That you can say a coach needs to do some things differently without saying he should be fired. And that he has never said the Chiefs should fire Andy Reid.

That is exactly right, it is exactly the standard on this channel, and I have said the same thing on this channel this week. Three Super Bowls earns enormous credit. It does not earn immunity. And nobody who points at fifteen point four percent from inside the five yard line is attacking a man's legacy.
