And I want to explain why that detail about the location matters so much, because it is the single most useful thing anybody has said about this injury in two months.

When an offensive lineman has a disc problem, it is almost always in the lower back. The lumbar spine. That is the part of the spine that absorbs load, and loading your spine is essentially the job description of an offensive lineman. You get into a stance, you take on three hundred pounds of resistance, and the force travels down through your lower back. So lumbar disc injuries in linemen are common, well understood, and there is a well-worn path for treating them.

What Burkholder described is a disc sitting higher than that. And higher up the spine, two things change.

First, the familiar roadmap does not apply as cleanly. The rehabilitation protocols, the loading progressions, the return-to-play benchmarks that medical staffs have refined over decades on lumbar discs are built for lumbar discs.

Second, and this is the part that explains the whole season, the nerves coming off that part of the spine serve different territory. Andy Reid said back in September that the disc was affecting the strength in Simmons's leg. Strength, not pain. And that distinction is everything.

Pain you can play through. Players play through pain every week of their careers. What you cannot play through is a strength deficit, because a left tackle whose leg will not fire on command is a left tackle who gets beaten around the edge, and the man he is protecting is coming off a rebuilt knee and cannot see it happening.

So when Burkholder says plateaued, he is not describing a man who stopped trying. He is describing nerve function that stopped improving. And nerve recovery is the single least negotiable thing in sports medicine. You cannot rush it, you cannot train it, you cannot want it into happening. You wait.

And before I go through the moves, let me explain how injured reserve actually works, because this is the mechanism the Chiefs have been managing around for seven weeks and almost nobody explains it.

When a team places a player on injured reserve during the season, that player comes off the fifty-three man active roster, which frees the spot for somebody who can actually play. He must then miss a minimum of four games. After that, if the team has designated him to return, he can begin practising again, and that opens a twenty-one day window. Inside those twenty-one days the team either activates him back onto the fifty-three or he stays on injured reserve for the rest of the season.

Now you can see exactly why Kansas City hesitated for seven weeks. Every time their medical staff believed Josh Simmons was two weeks away, placing him on injured reserve would have guaranteed he was away for four games instead. So they carried him and waited. And they did that again, and again, and again, and each individual decision was defensible.

What finally changed is not the calendar. It is the word plateaued. Once the recovery curve goes flat, the two-weeks-away bet stops making sense, and the four game minimum becomes the cheaper option. So they took the spot back.
