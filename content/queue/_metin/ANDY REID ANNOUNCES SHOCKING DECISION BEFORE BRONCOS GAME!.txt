Let’s start with what actually happened, in order, with the facts straight.

On Tuesday, the Kansas City Chiefs worked out three kickers. Three. Justin Tucker, Harrison Mevis, and Ben Sauls.

On Wednesday, October twenty-eighth, Ian Rapoport of NFL Network and Adam Schefter of ESPN both reported that the Chiefs had signed Justin Tucker.

And here is the detail that half the internet skipped right past, so let me put it in capital letters with my voice. He was signed to the practice squad. Not the active roster. The practice squad.

As of Wednesday morning, Harrison Butker was still the only kicker on the fifty-three man roster. And Andy Reid confirmed Butker is the guy on Sunday.

So before anybody tells you the Chiefs replaced their kicker, understand that they did not. What they did was open a competition and keep the incumbent’s job intact while they look at it.

Now let me explain why that distinction is not a technicality, because this is one of those pieces of roster plumbing that changes the whole story once you understand it.

A player on the practice squad is not on the game-day roster. He cannot just walk out of the tunnel on Sunday. To play, he has to be elevated to the active roster for that game, and every team gets a limited number of those elevations per player across a season, or the team has to sign him to the fifty-three outright, which means somebody else comes off it.

And those elevations are not unlimited. A team can elevate the same practice squad player a maximum of three times in a season. After the third one, if you still want him on game day, you have to sign him to the fifty-three, and then somebody who is on that roster today is not on it tomorrow.

So signing a kicker to the practice squad is the single lowest-commitment move available in professional football. It costs you a practice squad spot. That is it. It is not a vote of no confidence and it is not a replacement. It is an insurance policy sitting in a drawer.

Reid said that part himself, almost word for word. Quote, “He understands the business and how it works. We had a practice squad position open.”

And here is why the Chiefs felt they needed one, and I am not going to soften this, because the numbers do not allow it.

Harrison Butker has made eleven of sixteen field goal attempts this season. Eleven of sixteen. That is sixty-eight point eight percent. He missed two kicks in the loss in Seattle.

And it is not just this season. Since the start of last season, Harrison Butker has missed ten field goals and five extra points, and both of those are the most in the National Football League over that span. He is the third-highest-paid kicker in the league and he carries the sixth-highest base salary on the Kansas City Chiefs.

That is a problem. It has been a problem for more than a calendar year. And the thing I want you to sit with is this. Butker has been an elite kicker in this league. He has three Super Bowl rings. He has made kicks that are in the permanent highlight reel of this franchise. None of that is in dispute, and none of it makes a sixty-eight point eight percent season acceptable in a division race.

Now, Andy Reid’s handling of this was, I thought, a masterclass, and I want to read you the quotes, because he did something coaches almost never do. He told the truth about the player and protected the player at the same time.

On Butker himself, quote. “Listen, Harrison, we love. I’ve told you that before. He’s one of my favorite guys.”

On the performance, quote. “Obviously he didn’t do very good this past game. I’m stating the obvious there.” And then, quote, “and he’s been up and down. This happens.”

On what he expects now, quote. “Anybody else in this business, myself included, you have to pick it up and do better. He gets that.”

And then, quote, “sometimes things don’t go quite the way you want, and you work on that.”

And then the line that gave the whole press conference its headline, quote. “Butker gets it. He knows that’s part of the business. He’s struggling a little bit and he gets it.”

That is how you do it. No hiding behind the obvious, no throwing a ten-year veteran under a bus, no pretending a bad night was a good night. And then he announced the man is still kicking Sunday, which, after signing a seven-time Pro Bowler, is the genuinely surprising part of this whole week.

Now, the football case for Justin Tucker, and this is where I think a lot of the coverage has gone soft.

Justin Tucker spent thirteen seasons with the Baltimore Ravens. Seven Pro Bowls. A Super Bowl ring in his rookie season. For a stretch of years he was, by reasonable argument, the greatest kicker who has ever lived. Baltimore released him in May of twenty twenty-five with three years still left on a four-year, twenty-two-million-dollar extension that had made him the highest-paid kicker in football.

Now the part the highlight reels leave out.

In twenty twenty-four, his last season of professional football, Justin Tucker made twenty-two of thirty field goals. Twenty-two of thirty is seventy-three point three percent. That ranked thirty-first in the National Football League. He missed ten kicks that year, three more than in any season of his career.

He has not played in an NFL game since. He turns thirty-seven next month.

So let’s do the comparison honestly. Butker this season, sixty-eight point eight percent. Tucker in his last season, two years ago, seventy-three point three percent, thirty-first in the league.

That is not an upgrade. That is two stretches of unreliable kicking sitting next to each other.

And I want to credit the version of this story that landed in my inbox, because whoever wrote it got this exactly right and it deserves saying. Comparing a current season to a two-year-old season does not prove anything. The reputation has to match what the leg can still do today. Knowing how to handle the moment and actually making the kick are two different skills, and only one of them shows up on the scoreboard.

The Chiefs also worked out Harrison Mevis, and I want to spend thirty seconds on him, because he is the interesting name here.

Mevis kicked at Missouri, where they called him the Thiccer Kicker, and he has a genuinely unusual path. He kicked in the United Football League for the Birmingham Stallions, which got him signed by the New York Jets, and then he got to Los Angeles with the Rams, where he was promoted to the starting job before being released.

Why does that matter? Because the single best kicker story of the last few years is Brandon Aubrey, who Dallas found in that same spring-league pipeline, and who became one of the most dangerous legs in football. The raw version of this story mentioned Aubrey, and it mentioned that Kansas City once pulled Spencer Shrader out of a practice squad situation. Those examples do not prove there is a better kicker sitting available right now. What they prove is that the best available leg is not always the most famous name on the list.

I would have been fine with the Mevis direction. But I want to be very clear that that is a preference, not a prediction, and I cannot turn a Tuesday workout into a guaranteed solution any more than anybody else can.

Here is my honest expectation. Butker kicks Sunday, as Reid said. Tucker sits on the practice squad as leverage and as insurance. And we learn more from the next four field goal attempts than we learned from the entire news cycle.

Because here is the thing about a kicking slump that nobody on television explains, and it matters this week specifically.

A field goal is not one man. It is an operation. Snapper, holder, kicker. From the snap to the ball leaving the foot is roughly one and a third seconds. The holder’s spin, the laces, the tilt of the ball, the depth of the plant foot, the angle of the hip through contact. Change one element of that chain and you have changed the whole thing.

That is why coaches are so reluctant to swap kickers mid-season even when the percentages are ugly. You are not replacing a leg. You are rebuilding a timing mechanism, in-season, under playoff pressure.

And one more thing about Sunday specifically. People are going to tell you altitude in Denver helps kickers. It does, in terms of distance. Thinner air means less drag and the ball carries. But distance has not been Kansas City’s problem. Accuracy has. And a ball that carries farther also moves more off the intended line, which means Denver amplifies whatever is already happening with a kicker’s direction. Altitude is not a cure for a slump. It is a magnifying glass.

Now the genuinely good news, and it is a story that got buried under the kicker noise.

Omarr Norman-Lott has been designated to return to practice.

Let me give you the context, because this one has been a long wait. Norman-Lott was Kansas City’s second-round draft pick in twenty twenty-five, out of Tennessee, an interior pass rusher. He tore his ACL, and that injury ended his rookie season. He has been on the physically unable to perform list, and the Chiefs have now moved him off it.

Here is what that mechanism actually means, and it is worth knowing because this exact clock is going to matter in a few weeks. When a team designates a player to return from that list, it opens a twenty-one-day practice window. During those twenty-one days, he can practice with the team while still not counting against the fifty-three man roster. At the end of that window, the team has to make a decision. Activate him to the active roster, or put him on injured reserve for the rest of the season. There is no third option and there is no extension.

He was limited in practice. I am keeping my excitement measured and so should you. Returning to the field is not the same as being ready for sixty minutes of NFL football, and nobody should want the Chiefs to skip the steps in between. Let him build.

But think about why this matters right now, in this specific week. We just went through the sack numbers on this channel. Seven sacks as a team in six games, and not one player with more than one. An interior rusher who showed real pass-rush ability at Tennessee is exactly the shape of help this defense needs, and he is already in the building, already paid for, and does not cost a draft pick or a phone call to another general manager.

That is the cheapest reinforcement available to Brett Veach, and it does not require the trade deadline at all.
