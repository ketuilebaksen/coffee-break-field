Read that carefully, because Andy Reid chose his words very precisely and most people are going to miss why.

He did not say the officials were cheating. He did not say it was a conspiracy. He said he saw a head movement, and that a head movement is not a false start.

And on that narrow point, he is correct about the rule. A false start is any quick, abrupt movement by an offensive player who is in a set position that simulates the start of the play. The key words are simulates the start of the play. A lineman turning his head to look at something is not simulating a snap. What is a false start is shifting your stance, lifting a hand, rocking your weight forward. And the reporting on this one says Moore appeared to adjust his stance slightly with about ten seconds left on the play clock, and then turned his head.

So the honest version is this. There may have been two separate movements, and the question is which one the official saw and what he thought it was.

Now here is what you need to know, and it is the part nobody likes. A false start is a judgment call made by a live official in real time, and it is not reviewable. There is no replay for it, there is no appeal, there is no process by which the league can give Kansas City that touchdown back on Wednesday. The result is final. Now let me explain what a hairline fracture of the scapula means, because it is a more serious injury than the phrase sounds.

The scapula is your shoulder blade. It is the anchor. Every muscle that moves your arm overhead, every muscle that pulls it back, attaches to that bone. A hairline fracture means a crack that has not displaced, so the pieces are still lined up. The good news is that those usually heal without surgery. The bad news is the only treatment is time and rest.

And now think about what a cornerback does. He presses a receiver at the line with his hands and absorbs a two hundred pound man trying to run through him. He tackles. He gets blocked. And when he falls at full speed, which happens a dozen times a game, he lands on that shoulder or braces with that arm. There is no version of playing cornerback that rests a shoulder blade.

So that shoulder has been a problem since the offseason program, it wiped out most of his training camp, he aggravated it in week one against Denver on the interception he caught, and now we know there is a crack in the bone. And one more. Peter Woods, the first-round rookie defensive tackle, went down early in Denver with a neck injury and was ruled out for the rest of the game. The reporting describes it as a stinger, and he is now pointed toward the Jets game.

Let me tell you what a stinger actually is, because the name makes it sound trivial and it is not.

A stinger is a stretch or compression injury to the brachial plexus, which is the bundle of nerves that runs from your neck through your shoulder and down your arm. It usually happens on a collision that forces the head and shoulder apart. The sensation is burning and numbness shooting down one arm, and the arm goes weak. Most of them resolve in minutes or hours and most players return. But you cannot play football with an arm you cannot trust, because a defensive lineman's entire job is shedding blocks with his hands. That is why they take you out immediately.

Good news on it being described as a stinger rather than something structural. But the words neck injury and ruled out in the same sentence always deserve respect.
