And let me explain what a restructure actually is, because it is the single most used tool in this sport and most fans have never had it explained.

A player's cap number in any year is made up of several parts, and the two that matter here are base salary and signing bonus. Base salary counts against the cap entirely in the year it is paid. A signing bonus is different. It gets spread evenly across the life of the contract, up to five years.

So a restructure works like this. You take a big chunk of a player's base salary for this season, you convert it into a signing bonus, and you pay him exactly the same money at exactly the same time. He does not lose a cent and he does not gain one. But instead of all of that counting against this year's cap, it now counts a fifth at a time across five years.

The result is immediate space. Convert fifteen million of base salary into bonus on a five year deal and you have just created twelve million of room today.

And the catch, which is the whole catch, is that the other four fifths did not disappear. They moved into future seasons. So every restructure makes this year easier and every future year harder, and if you do it enough times you end up with a cap full of money owed to players for games they already played. That is how franchises get into trouble.

Which is why the name mentioned most often here is Creed Humphrey. He is young, he is signed long term, and he is the sort of player you can comfortably push money into the future for, because you are confident he will still be worth it when the bill arrives.

And since I keep using the phrase prove-it deal with incentives, let me be precise about what that means, because it is the structure that makes this possible at all.

A contract's cap hit is driven by guaranteed money. If you promise a player ten million dollars no matter what, most of that lands on your cap immediately. But if you promise him a modest base and then attach bonuses to things he has to actually achieve, the accounting changes completely.

Incentives get split into two categories. If a bonus is considered likely to be earned, based on the previous season's production, it counts against this year's cap. If it is considered not likely to be earned, it does not count this year at all. It only lands later, after he earns it.

Now apply that to a thirty-two year old who caught twenty-one passes last season before his knee gave out. Almost any meaningful production target for Tyreek Hill in twenty twenty-six would be classified as not likely to be earned, because his most recent season was four games long.

Which means Kansas City could offer him a genuinely attractive package, with real money available if he returns to form, and have it cost very little against this season's three point three million. He gets paid if he produces. They pay nothing if he does not.

That is why this is even a conversation with the cap room they have. Not because the Chiefs found a loophole, but because the player's recent production is so limited that the accounting treats his upside as hypothetical.
