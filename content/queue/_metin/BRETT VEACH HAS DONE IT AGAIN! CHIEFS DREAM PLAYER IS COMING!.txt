Forty-two and a half million dollars.

That is the number that ends the Terry McLaurin conversation, and I have not heard a single person say it out loud.

Chiefs Kingdom wants Terry McLaurin. I understand completely. He’s one of the best route runners in football, he’s stuck on a team going nowhere, and the idea of handing him to Patrick Mahomes is genuinely intoxicating.

And the proposal I keep seeing is a third-round pick and a sixth. Clean. Reasonable. Let the man go win something.

So I went and actually looked up his contract. And the draft picks were never the problem. The draft picks were never anywhere close to the problem.

Today I’m going to show you exactly why this trade cannot happen, why that matters for every other name you’re going to hear before November tenth, and then I’m going to give you the list of receivers who are genuinely available, with real prices.

What’s up everybody, welcome back to Coffee Break on the Field. Pour the coffee. Let’s do the math nobody wants to do.

First, let me make the case for him properly, because I am not here to tell you the idea is stupid. On the field, it’s a very good idea.

Terry McLaurin is an outstanding football player. The route running is the best part of his game, and it’s the kind of skill that doesn’t decline the way raw speed does. He has excellent hands. He gets open against good defenses, consistently, and he has done it for years with quarterback situations that ranged from mediocre to genuinely bad.

Look at his résumé. Multiple thousand-yard seasons. A thirteen-touchdown year in twenty twenty-four. He has been the one thing that worked in Washington for most of his career.

And this season, even in a bad spot, the signal is still there. He’s around two hundred yards so far, and against Seattle, which is a legitimately top defense, he put up six catches for seventy-seven yards and a touchdown. Against the Giants, four catches for sixty-two yards.

Last season he only played ten games, and in those ten he had five hundred and eighty-two yards and three touchdowns.

So let’s be clear about what we’re discussing. Terry McLaurin is not washed. Terry McLaurin is wasted. Those are completely different diagnoses, and the second one is exactly the kind of player a contender should be hunting.

And the emotional argument is real too. Washington is struggling, Jayden Daniels has had real trouble staying on the field, and McLaurin has spent his entire career being brilliant for teams that couldn’t use it.

So yes. On football grounds, I’d love him in Kansas City. Let’s say that plainly before I take it away from you.

And picture the fit for a second, because it really is the cleanest one available.

Kansas City needs somebody who separates against man coverage. That has been the specific failure of this receiver room for two years now. Xavier Worthy wins with speed, which is a different skill. Rashee Rice wins underneath. What this offense has not had since Tyreek Hill left is a man who simply beats his defender with footwork, on any route, at any depth.

That is precisely what Terry McLaurin is. And it’s the skill that ages best, which is why a thirty-year-old McLaurin would still work in January.

Put him opposite Worthy, with Travis Kelce in the middle and Kenneth Walker forcing a safety down, and you have genuinely no good way to defend it.

So I want you to understand I’m not arguing against the football. The football is perfect. I’m arguing against the spreadsheet.

Now here’s the contract.

In August of twenty twenty-five, Terry McLaurin signed a three-year extension with Washington worth up to roughly ninety-seven million dollars, with about forty-four point six five million guaranteed at signing.

His twenty twenty-six salary cap hit is eighteen million dollars. His twenty twenty-seven cap hit is thirty-four million dollars.

And if Washington trades him this year, they absorb approximately forty-two and a half million dollars in dead cap.

Let me explain dead cap in plain language, because it’s the single most misunderstood thing in football and it’s where ninety percent of fan trade ideas go to die.

When a team signs a player, a big chunk of the money is a signing bonus. For cap purposes, that bonus gets spread out across the years of the contract. So you pay the player up front, but you only count a slice of it each season.

The moment that player leaves, all the remaining slices accelerate onto your current books at once. And here’s the brutal part. You are now carrying that money against your salary cap for a player who is not on your roster, who is scoring touchdowns for somebody else, on a Sunday, on television.

Forty-two and a half million dollars.

For context, that is more than twice the entire three-year contract Kansas City gave Kenneth Walker.

So the Washington Commanders would be paying forty-two and a half million dollars to watch Terry McLaurin play for the Kansas City Chiefs. In exchange for a third-round pick.

No general manager in the history of the National Football League does that in the middle of October.

And it doesn’t get better on Kansas City’s side either. You’d be taking on an eighteen million dollar cap hit this season, and then a thirty-four million dollar cap hit next season, for a receiver who turned thirty last month.

That’s the whole thing. The picks were never the obstacle.

Okay. Quick pause, because I want to turn this into something useful rather than just disappointing, since you’re going to hear fifteen more of these before the deadline.

Here’s a rule you can use for the rest of your life as a football fan.

Before you propose a trade, look up the dead cap.

Not the draft pick. The dead cap. If the dead money the selling team would eat is larger than what they’d save in salary, that trade does not exist. It doesn’t matter how badly the player wants out, how bad the team is, or how fair the pick feels.

Draft picks are the only currency fans can see. The cap is invisible, and the cap is where these ideas actually die.

And there’s a second number worth sitting with, because it tells you why Washington structured it this way in the first place.

His twenty twenty-seven cap hit is thirty-four million dollars. That’s a genuinely large number, and it’s the year where this contract starts to hurt Washington if McLaurin declines.

Which means there is a world where he becomes available. It just isn’t this one. The page I pulled his numbers from shows a potential exit in twenty twenty-seven with about eighteen million in dead cap instead of forty-two and a half.

Eighteen million is painful. Forty-two and a half is impossible. That’s the difference between a trade a rebuilding team might swallow and a trade nobody on earth makes.

So if you genuinely love Terry McLaurin in a Kansas City uniform, the honest answer is that you’re looking at the wrong calendar year. Ask me again next spring.

And now let me correct the premise underneath this whole thing, because I’ve heard it repeated all week and it’s backwards.

The argument goes: Washington wouldn’t mind letting him go. He’s been loyal, he’s been suffering, they should just let him move on.

They paid him ninety-seven million dollars thirteen months ago.

Teams do not hand a man the largest contract in franchise history at his position and then dump him for a day-two pick a year later. That signature is the single clearest statement a franchise can make about a player’s future. They didn’t just keep him. They committed.

And look at the comparisons people are making, because they actually prove the opposite point.

A.J. Brown left Tennessee because the Titans wouldn’t pay him the extension he wanted. Saquon Barkley left the Giants because they let him walk rather than commit. Derrick Henry left in free agency at the end of his deal.

Every single one of those is a story about a team declining to pay. McLaurin’s story is a team that already paid.

That’s not a technicality. That is the exact reason he’s unavailable. The commitment that makes him valuable is the same commitment that makes him immovable.

Alright. So let’s talk about what’s actually on the board, with prices, because this part is genuinely useful.

There’s one more thing I want to say about the McLaurin fantasy, and it’s about the version of the argument I actually agree with.

Strip away the name and what people are really asking for is modest. Nobody watching this is demanding Justin Jefferson. Nobody is asking for Jaxon Smith-Njigba or Puka Nacua. The actual request is simpler than that.

Give Patrick Mahomes one more receiver who can get open and produce seventy yards on a Sunday.

That’s it. That’s the whole ask, and it is completely reasonable. The frustration isn’t greed. It’s watching the best quarterback of his generation work with a receiver room that has been a crisis in four straight seasons while everyone else has somebody.

So I’m not dismissing the feeling. I’m telling you the particular door you’re knocking on is welded shut, and pointing at three that aren’t.

Name one. Jauan Jennings of Minnesota.

Look at the recent production. Seventy-seven catches for nine hundred and seventy-five yards and six touchdowns in twenty twenty-four with San Francisco. Fifty-five catches for six hundred and forty-three yards and nine touchdowns last season. Nine touchdowns.

Now look at Minnesota. Through four weeks he has played three games, started one, and caught two passes for nine yards on four targets. The Vikings then signed Odell Beckham Jr. after the Giants released him, and I want to be precise, Beckham went to the practice squad, not the active roster. But local Minnesota analysts have read it as a signal anyway. Jud Zulgad on Purple Daily said, it feels like he’s not a fit.

And here’s the Kansas City connection. Adam Schefter reported back in May that the Chiefs and the Commanders were both interested in Jennings before he signed in Minnesota. So this front office has already done the homework on him.

Price: a day-three pick plus the prorated salary. And I want to be clear, there are no confirmed trade talks. This is analysis, not a report.

But the fit is the thing I keep coming back to. Jennings is a genuinely good blocker on the perimeter. On a team that leads the National Football League in rushing, with Kenneth Walker at five hundred and thirty-seven yards, a receiver who will put his face on a cornerback isn’t a nice bonus. He’s a multiplier on your best unit.

And one honest flag on Jennings before I move on, because I’ve been recommending him for a week and I should say the uncomfortable part.

Two catches for nine yards in three games is not a slump you explain away. Minnesota paid him a base salary around eight million dollars this year and has essentially stopped throwing him the football. Teams usually have a reason.

The optimistic read is that he’s a scheme casualty in an offense that doesn’t use him the way San Francisco did, and that nine touchdowns last season is closer to the truth than nine yards this season. I lean that way. But I’d be selling you something if I pretended the Vikings’ usage wasn’t a warning sign, and it’s exactly why the price has to stay on day three.

Name two. Jerry Jeudy of Cleveland.

And the reason I keep him on this list is specific. Chad O’Shea, Kansas City’s new wide receivers coach this season, came here from the Browns, where he was Jerry Jeudy’s position coach. The man who knows how to get the best out of him is already in the building, which short-circuits the thing that kills most midseason receiver trades.

The production is a problem. Jeudy had a Pro Bowl season in twenty twenty-four with ninety catches and over a thousand yards, then collapsed. This year he’s at five catches for sixty-two yards through four games with a fifty-seven percent snap share, and younger Cleveland receivers are ahead of him.

A conditional fifth-round pick, I’m picking up the phone. A fourth that becomes a third, I’m passing.

Name three. Brian Thomas Jr. of Jacksonville.

As a rookie he put up one thousand two hundred and eighty-two yards and ten touchdowns. That’s a ceiling we have already seen. He’s six foot four with an enormous catch radius.

The problems are real. Last season he had ten drops and missed three games. This season, through five games, eighteen targets, nine catches, a hundred and five yards, one touchdown, with only one game above sixty percent of the offensive snaps.

And Jacksonville has said they are not trading him and called the suggestion, in their own word, fraudulent. They’re also a direct AFC rival, which adds a premium on top of any price.

Houston just set the market for a fallen first-rounder when they gave Arizona a twenty twenty-seven second and a sixth for Marvin Harrison Jr. So if the ask is a second, I pass. If somebody says a third, and Kansas City is projected to have two of those next year, I listen hard, because he’s twenty-three and under contract through twenty twenty-seven. That’s not renting eleven games. That’s buying a receiver for the middle of the Mahomes extension.

And then the free options, which are the ones that actually matter this Sunday.

Justin Watson is on the practice squad with three available elevations. From twenty twenty-two through twenty twenty-four his average depth of target was seventeen point two yards, and fifty-six of his sixty-four Chiefs catches went for a first down or a touchdown.

Jalen Royals has three catches on the season.

And Cyrus Allen has ten offensive snaps. Ten, in four games.

And before I get to that, one more free option that deserves saying out loud, because it’s the one nobody mentions and it costs the least of all.

Kenneth Walker and Emmett Johnson as receivers out of the backfield.

If Andy Reid genuinely doesn’t trust his young wideouts yet, he has other ways to move the football. More two and three tight end sets. Screens and swing routes to a running back who is averaging six point two yards a carry and already leads the league in rushing. A defense that has to account for Walker on a handoff and then watch him leak into the flat is a defense playing eleven against twelve.

That’s not a long-term answer to a receiver problem. But it’s an answer for Sunday, and it doesn’t require anybody’s permission or a single draft pick.

Which brings me to the sharpest point I’ve heard made about this team all week, and I want to credit it because it’s correct and a little uncomfortable.

With Andy Reid, it always takes an injury.

Think about it. Tyquan Thornton got his opportunity because of injuries. He took it and turned into this offense’s best deep threat. And now Cyrus Allen is getting his opportunity, and the only reason is that Thornton is on injured reserve and Rashee Rice didn’t practice Wednesday with a hamstring.

That’s a real pattern, and as a criticism of how this staff distributes snaps, it lands.

But I want to give you the other half of it, because it’s the same coin.

The reason Kansas City keeps finding players out of nowhere is that same mechanism. Tyquan Thornton was signed to the practice squad two years ago, reportedly three days after New England cut him. A discarded player. And in Las Vegas he caught five passes for a hundred and eleven yards and two touchdowns in the best game of his life.

So the thing that frustrates you in October, a coach who won’t give a young player snaps until he’s forced to, is the same thing that produces a Thornton when he is forced to. I’d still like to see them move faster. But the record on what happens when they finally do is pretty good.

And Sunday there is no excuse left. Rice is out, Thornton is on IR, and Reid has publicly said Allen will get more opportunities. An zero and four opponent at home is the cheapest possible place to find out what you have.

So here’s where I land, and let me put the whole picture together.

And there’s one more reason I’d be cautious about spending big at this deadline that has nothing to do with receivers.

Kansas City’s actual weakness isn’t the passing game. It’s that this defense has four sacks in four games, all of them from week one, with Chris Jones and George Karlaftis at zero. They rank twelfth in the league in pressure rate and thirty-first in sack rate.

Now, I’ve argued for weeks that this corrects itself, and I still believe that, especially with Omarr Norman-Lott eligible to come off the physically unable to perform list. But if it doesn’t correct itself by early November, that is where I’d want the draft capital going. Not a fourth receiver.

A pass rush that can’t finish in January loses playoff games. A receiver room that’s a bit thin while you lead the league in explosive plays does not. If Veach spends a real pick before November tenth, I’d honestly rather he spent it on the defensive line.

Over the last two weeks, Brett Veach has shown you his price four separate times. He declined a second-round pick for Marvin Harrison Jr. and let Houston pay it. He signed a thirty-year-old receiver to a practice squad contract instead of the fifty-three. He elevated a special teams linebacker. And he has not rushed into anything on Tyreek Hill.

That’s not passivity. That’s a man who decided what a receiver is worth and has refused to move off it, four times, while sitting at four and oh.

And Terry McLaurin isn’t even a price question. It’s an arithmetic question, and the arithmetic says no. Forty-two and a half million dollars in dead money for the team that would have to sell him. That trade doesn’t exist in any universe.

What I’d actually do is unchanged. Elevate Justin Watson Sunday and use all three. Put Cyrus Allen on the field and find out. And keep a day-three pick ready for Jauan Jennings or Jerry Jeudy before November tenth if either price comes down.

And remember what this team actually is while you’re wanting more. Four and oh. A turnover margin of plus five, tied for second in football. The league’s leading rusher. A quarterback who just tied Tom Brady’s record for wins through a hundred and thirty starts. The most explosive-play offense in the NFL by the counting stats, with six receptions of forty or more yards and fourteen plays of twenty-five or more.

They are doing all of that without a number one receiver. That’s not a crisis. That’s a team that built itself differently on purpose.

One last thing to watch before kickoff. The Friday injury report and the game-day inactives.

Three names decide how this offense looks on Sunday. Is Rashee Rice ruled out, which would confirm Worthy as the only established receiver on the field. Is Justin Watson elevated, which starts his three-game clock. And does Josh Simmons dress after practicing for the first time all season.

Those three lines tell you more about Sunday than any trade rumor will.
