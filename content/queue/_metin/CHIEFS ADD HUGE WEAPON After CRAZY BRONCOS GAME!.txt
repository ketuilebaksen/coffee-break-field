And Walder gave one more number which is the damning one. Chris Godwin is averaging just one point two yards per route run this season.

Let me explain that statistic, because it is the single best measure of receiver efficiency that exists and almost no broadcast uses it.

Yards per route run does not ask how many times a quarterback threw to you. It asks: every single time you ran a route, how many yards did the offense get out of you? It includes all the plays where you were not targeted, which is the point, because a receiver who runs forty routes and is only worth looking at on six of them is not doing his job, even if those six go well.

The elite number in the National Football League is somewhere around two and a half to three yards per route run. A solid starter sits around one point eight to two. Anything at or below one point three is replacement level.

One point two is below that line.

So on the pure football merits, Heavy's write-up of Walder's idea said it best, and I agree with it. Chris Godwin, quote, is not likely a needle mover.

And there is an injury file too. Chris Godwin has missed seventeen games over the past two seasons. Seventeen. His decline has been tied to both the injuries and inconsistent quarterback play around him, and the second part is the strongest argument in his favor, because inconsistent quarterback play is not a problem he would have in Kansas City.

But now let me get to why I actually brought this up, because the contract mechanism here is the most useful thing in this video and it applies to every name on the board.

Chris Godwin is on a fully guaranteed twenty-two million dollar salary.

Twenty-two million. Fully guaranteed. And Kansas City has roughly one point two five million dollars in cap space.

On its face, that is impossible. It is not even close to possible. So how does anybody propose it with a straight face?

Here is how. Walder pointed out that Tampa Bay would likely convert most of Godwin's remaining salary into a signing bonus before trading him.

Let me walk you through what that actually does, because it is the single most important piece of deadline plumbing in football and once you know it you will understand ninety percent of the trades that happen on Tuesday.

When a player's salary is paid as base salary, it counts against the cap of the team that pays it, week by week. When it is converted into a signing bonus, it gets paid out immediately by the team doing the converting, and the cap charge for that bonus stays with that team, spread across the remaining years of the deal.

So if Tampa Bay converts twenty million of Godwin's remaining salary into a signing bonus, Tampa Bay writes the check and Tampa Bay keeps the cap charge. What transfers to Kansas City is whatever base salary is left, which could be close to the league minimum.

In other words: the selling team eats the money in exchange for getting a draft pick, and the buying team gets a player it could never otherwise afford.

That is why you will see teams with no cap space acquire expensive players at a deadline and everybody acts confused. It is not magic. It is a seller agreeing to absorb the cost of its own decision to sell.

And it means the one point two five million figure, which I have been quoting to you for three days as a constraint, is softer than it looks, as long as the selling team is motivated. A seller who wants picks will restructure to make the deal happen. A seller who does not care will tell you to absorb the whole contract and the conversation ends.

So keep that in mind for every name I am about to give you. The question is never only "can Kansas City afford him." It is "does the other team want to sell badly enough to pay for the privilege."

And there is a second half to that mechanism that nobody explains, which is why a seller would ever agree to it.

Put yourself in Tampa Bay's chair. Chris Godwin's twenty-two million dollars this season is fully guaranteed, which means they owe him every cent of it whether he plays for them, plays for somebody else, or sits at home. That money is gone either way. It is already spent.

So the only live question for them is what they get in exchange for money they cannot recover. If they keep him, they get a receiver past his prime on a team going nowhere. If they convert the salary, eat the cap charge and trade him, they get a draft pick for free, because the cash was leaving the building regardless.

That is why guaranteed money makes a player more tradable at a deadline rather than less, which is the exact opposite of what most fans assume. The guarantee removes the seller's incentive to hold on. And it is the same reason Calvin Ridley's largely unguaranteed future money makes him cheap, and the same reason Garrett Wilson's one hundred thirty million is untouchable: nobody is converting a deal that runs to twenty thirty for a ninth-place draft pick.

Learn that one principle and you can read every deadline rumor for the rest of your life.

And there is a historical pattern worth knowing here, because this franchise has a very consistent habit at deadlines.

Brett Veach is not, historically, a deadline blockbuster general manager. His big swings happen in March and April, in free agency and in the draft, where he has the full cap year and the full pick board to work with. At deadlines he tends to make small, targeted additions: a depth lineman, a rotational defender, a veteran receiver on a minimum deal. The kind of move that does not make a thumbnail.
