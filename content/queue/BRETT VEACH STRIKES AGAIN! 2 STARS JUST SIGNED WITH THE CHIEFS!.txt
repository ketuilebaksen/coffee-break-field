Now, here is the thing you have to understand about Brett Veach, and it explains everything that comes next.

Veach does not fall in love. When he looks at a player, he sees two things at the same time. He sees the football player, and he sees a number. What that player costs this year. What he will cost next year. And, most importantly, what that same money would buy somewhere else on the roster.

Fans hate that. Of course they do. Falling in love with players is literally what being a fan is. That is the whole point. But a general manager who falls in love is a general manager who runs out of money, and a team that runs out of money runs out of seasons.

So that winter, Veach sat down with a problem that had no pretty answer. A quarterback rehabbing a knee. Holes at cornerback, at safety, at tackle, on the defensive line, and in the backfield. And almost no cap space to fix any of it.

Two roads.

Road one is the safe road. Keep everybody. Patch the holes with cheap veterans. Tell the fan base you had bad injury luck and pray the quarterback comes back healthy. Nobody ever gets fired for taking road one.

Road two has no guardrail on it. Tear a piece out of the heart of your own defense on purpose. Turn it into draft capital. Get younger, get faster, get cheaper, and bet your entire reputation on your ability to hit on those picks. If it works, you are a genius. Now the draft itself, because this is the part of the story that is genuinely thrilling, and it is the part where Veach did the exact opposite of what everyone expected.

Kansas City was sitting at number nine overall. That is a high pick. That is the kind of pick you only get when your season has gone badly wrong, and for this franchise it was the first time in a very long time they had been anywhere near the top of the board.

Veach looked at the board, looked at the eight teams picking in front of him, and felt something he later admitted out loud. Urgency.

And let me explain what a man coverage grade actually measures, because I think people hear a number like ninety-three point eight and nod along without knowing what it is.

Zone coverage is the forgiving one. In zone, you have help. You have a safety over the top, you have a linebacker underneath, and if you get beaten by half a step there is usually somebody behind you to clean it up. A zone corner can have a bad rep and nobody ever finds out.

Man coverage has nowhere to hide. It is you and him. He knows where he is going and you do not. He gets to plant and break and you have to react to it. If you lose, there is no safety net, there is just a receiver running free and a camera finding your face. Man coverage grades are the purest measurement in all of defensive football of whether a cornerback can actually cover a human being by himself.

And a rookie who missed training camp is leading the entire National Football League in it.
