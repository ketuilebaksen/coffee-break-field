And let me explain what a three down receiver actually means, because Solak used that phrase and it is the key to understanding what Kansas City is missing.

Most receivers in this league are specialists, even the good ones. One man is a vertical threat who runs nine routes and clears out space. Another is a slot receiver who lives on option routes against linebackers. Another is a possession receiver who moves the chains on third down and does not stretch anybody.

A three down receiver is a player you never have to take off the field, because he can do all of it. He can win on a slant on first down, run a seam on second, and beat man coverage on third and seven. And crucially, a defence cannot tell anything from his presence, because he does not announce the play just by being out there.

Now look at what Kansas City actually has. Xavier Worthy is extraordinary with the ball in space and down the field, and he is twenty-three and still learning. Travis Kelce is a tight end, and at thirty-seven he is still the best intermediate target on the roster. Jalen Royals and Cyrus Allen are rookies. Andrew Armstrong was on the practice squad four weeks ago.

So Solak's diagnosis is right. The hole is not speed and it is not depth. It is one man who can be targeted ten times a game at every level of the field without the offence telling the defence what is coming.

And here is the honest historical problem with every single one of these ideas, which almost nobody raises because it is not fun.

Mid-season receiver trades rarely work.

Not never. But rarely, and the reason is simple and it is not about talent. It is about time.

A receiver's value to a quarterback is built on shared reps. Where he sits down on a hook route against this coverage versus that one. How he adjusts a route when a safety rotates late. Whether he keeps running when the quarterback breaks the pocket, and where he goes when he does. None of that is in a playbook. It lives in a thousand practice repetitions and a quarterback's instinct about what a specific human being will do.

Patrick Mahomes explained that himself earlier this month, when he described how he built chemistry with Travis Kelce. He said Kelce used to talk to him after every single play. Every play, for a decade. That is what trust at this position actually costs.

So a receiver arriving on the eleventh of November has nine regular season games to build something that normally takes two years, in the middle of a season, without a training camp, while learning one of the most complicated offences in football.

Which is exactly why the names that keep surfacing around this team are players who have already caught passes from Patrick Mahomes. Familiarity is not a tiebreaker at this time of year. It is most of the value.

And it is why I keep saying the realistic version of a Kansas City deadline move is a cheap depth addition rather than a star, no matter how exciting the napkin looks.
